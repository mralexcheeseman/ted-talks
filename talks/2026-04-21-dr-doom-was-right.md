## Dr. Doom Was Right

*Being right and being on time are not the same thing.*

**Theme:** business

**Source idea:** Most strategic mistakes, she argues, are not failures of intelligence. They are failures of temporal positioning. Smart people applying the right thinking at the wrong moment.

---

**01 — THE HOOK [0:00 – 0:35]**

In March 2000, a man named Tony Dye cleaned out his desk at a London fund management firm. For three years he had refused to buy technology stocks. The press called him Dr. Doom. His clients had pulled billions.

Days later, the NASDAQ peaked and began the worst crash in a generation.

He was right. He was also unemployed.

That's not a story about bad luck. That's the flight data recorder of almost every strategic failure I've ever studied.

> **SLIDE:** *Black-and-white photograph, slightly grainy: a middle-aged man in a suit, late afternoon light, standing at an office window with a cardboard box on the desk behind him. No text.*

---

**02 — THE PAIN POINT [0:35 – 1:10]**

We tell ourselves strategic failures are failures of intelligence. Bad analysis. Wrong thinking.

They almost never are.

Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975. Nokia had a touchscreen smartphone prototype in 2004. Blockbuster was offered Netflix for fifty million dollars and laughed the founders out of the room.

They weren't wrong. They were early, then late. Never on time.

There's a name for this. Temporal misalignment. Smart people applying the right thinking at the wrong moment.

And here's what makes it cruel: it looks identical to being wrong. From the outside, nobody can tell the difference.

> **SLIDE:** *White screen. Three dates stacked in black: "1975 — Kodak." "2000 — Blockbuster." "2004 — Nokia." Below, in smaller grey text: "Not wrong. Early, then late."*

---

**03 — THE SURPRISE [1:10 – 1:50]**

Here's the part most people miss.

When a strategy fails, we conduct an autopsy of the thinking. What did they get wrong? We should be asking a different question. What time was it when they were right?

Being early is not a smaller version of being correct. It is a different category of error. The person who saw the 2000 crash in 1996 and the person who missed it entirely — they both lost their jobs.

This is the reversal nobody teaches: in strategy, timing is not an attribute of intelligence. It is a replacement for it.

A mediocre idea in the right month beats a brilliant idea in the wrong one. Every time.

> **SLIDE:** *Large white text on black: "Timing is not an attribute of intelligence." Below, the same sentence again — with "attribute" crossed out in red and replaced with "replacement."*

---

**04 — THE RUSH OF DOING NOTHING [1:50 – 2:20]**

When you learn this, every instinct tells you to do the opposite of nothing. Move faster. Ship sooner. Read the market harder.

Don't.

The thing to sit with first is how much of your past has been misdiagnosed. The idea you were told was bad. The plan you abandoned. The warning you gave that made you look paranoid.

Some of those weren't failures of thinking. They were right at the wrong time.

And you spent years believing you were the problem.

You need to mourn that before you do anything else. Not optimize it. Mourn it.

> **SLIDE:** *Black screen. Silence. Hold.*

---

**05 — THE PATH [2:20 – 2:50]**

Three moves.

Before you commit to a strategy, ask what time it is. Not calendar time — market time, organizational time, cultural time. What year is the room you're walking into actually living in?

When you're right too early, don't abandon the idea. Shelve it with a date. Early is just unripe.

Stop judging your past ideas by their outcomes. Judge them by their timing. That is how you learn what your real signal is.

> **SLIDE:** *Three numbered lines, white on black: "1. What time is it?" "2. Shelve it with a date." "3. Judge by timing, not outcome."*

---

**06 — THE CLOSE [2:50 – 3:00]**

Tony Dye died in 2008. His obituaries all said the same thing: he was right.

He was also eight years too early to keep his job.

The smartest person in the room isn't the one who sees the future. It's the one who knows what time it is.
